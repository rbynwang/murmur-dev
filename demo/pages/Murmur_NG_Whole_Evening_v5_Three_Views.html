The evening cut, the money, and what the tests found

An aggregator such as ENGIE asks for a lower evening peak, and three kinds of participant deliver it: a community through its community agent, a data center, and a solar-and-battery plant whose battery is under a peak contract.

One business: the evening cut

The same request, answer and report serve every participant.

What is soldBeing lower at the highest hours, by the kW. A home may also push stored power out to its neighbours
Who delivers itCommunities: homes and buildings, with their batteries, cars and devices that can wait. Data centers: batteries, and work that can wait. Solar-and-battery plants: batteries under a peak contract
Who paysWhoever bears the cost of the peak: a grid operator or a local utility
On ENGIE's dataMeasured: 96 strained hours to 21, with every battery held for the peak. No price for a peak kW is given
What stays outsideElectricity itself. Every participant buys and sells it as it does today

The order of asking

  1. Batteries, all together: the plants', the data centers' and the homes'
  2. The data centers' work that can wait
  3. Homes' other devices and buildings, where people have to be paid

The money, in plain words

Three words, kept apart.

Bill
What a participant pays for the electricity it uses. It exists without us.
Sales
What a participant earns for electricity it sends out. It exists without us.
Flexibility income
What a participant is paid for the evening cut. New money, and only where someone pays for the peak.

The figures

  • Flexibility income of a home with the full device mix: $1,249 a year at $2.04 per kWh over 45 evenings. That price is our own assumption: a Massachusetts summer programme's $275 per kW, which is $1.53 to $3.06 per kWh by how often it calls.
  • The same home at prices nearer ENGIE's region: about $459 a year at the $0.75 per kWh an Illinois utility has paid its households, and about $330 to $360 at the Midwest capacity price.
  • The split we assume: 70% to the members, 10% to the community's operator, 20% to the aggregator.
  • ENGIE's data gives no price for a peak kW, so every dollar here rests on a price from elsewhere.

What the tests found

Each part checked alone, on ENGIE's 2025 records.

  • The site agent equals the plan's replay for all 60 single sites, every quarter hour of 2025. Three of them have run on NANDA Town beside a community, every report matching.
  • The plan needs no price. Reading only the grid's loading it leaves 19 strained hours, against 21. Without the plants' batteries it leaves 84: they hold three quarters of the battery power.
  • Sharing a refusal out again adds nothing: with 1 site in 20 holding back, 23 strained hours without the round and 23 with it. Every battery is already asked for all it has.
  • The plan is nearly immune to false reports of charge: if every participant overstated every evening, 23 strained hours against 21. The batteries are full when the plan is made.
  • A language model was tried at the request agent: 40 of 40 requests written as they should be, 30 of 40 operators' notes read exactly right. A request is a list of numbers, so the request agent is plain code.
  • The community agent does not change what is delivered or paid. It makes the promise honest: the true shortfall is 2.7% with it and 16.9% without.
  • Data centers' work that can wait, about 54 MW, takes the 21 remaining strained hours only to 17: the worst transformer barely responds to them.
  • At ENGIE's own energy prices the homes' evening cut is worth about $263 an evening for a 200-home community. The home and building side needs a price for the peak.
